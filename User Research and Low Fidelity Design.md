## Target Audience  
This website is designed to focus on university students as its main targets as it allows them to get help with study or even tasks around their home by performing the same services to other students.  

This targets any university students that go to any kind of university although the concept will mostly be limited to other students at the user's current university since they most likely will be in person for the tutoring. Some exceptions can be made with tutoring done online.  

The reason that university students are the focus is because whilst they are doing university, they also have a lot to do outside of that creating a lot of stress as well as university being harder than what they have previously dealt with.

## Scope Statement  
The key aspect of the website is a token-based transactional service. The way this will function is that when students give out their services (e.g. helping someone with math's, programming, cleaning, creating meals etc.) they gain a token which in return allows them to buy a service from another student.  

There will need to be a database/storage solution to keep and show the number of tokens a student has as well as a program that removes and adds these tokens depending on whether they are buying or selling a service.

# User Research Report  

## Research Methods  
To make Flinders Uni Skill Share (FUSS) truly valuable, the project needed to first understand the university students who would be using the service. To avoid pointless guessing about what features students might want, several techniques were used to find out about student needs, preferences, and concerns.  

### 1. Online Survey  
An online survey was widely distributed among students via different club mailing lists and course group chats. The goal was to get a broad picture of what skills students felt confident in offering, what types of help they would most likely request for, and any concerns they would have regardinbg their participation in a peer-to-peer exchange. This information gathered was to serve the purpose of providing quantitative data about services that would probably be in high demand and about how much time stude...

### 2. Semi-Structured Interviews  
Following up on just yes/no sort of answers from surveys, several interviews were conducted with students from many areas of study and years in uni. These chats helped bring out individual experiences, such as informal tutoring among friends, or situations where students found it hard to get any trustworthy help from people on campus. A further exploration addressed perceptions of equity and trust, along with how students would want to organize the timing of such services.  

### 3. Competitor and Related Platform Analysis  
We reviewed existing systems (such as local "time bank" initiatives, skill-swapping websites, and university tutoring programs) and identified some common strengths (e.g. credit systems and review features), and some common traps (e.g. inactive users and the lack of anything to build trust). These were informative and gave us a sense of what FUSS should aim to replicate and what we should try to avoid.  

### 4. Focus Groups with Prototypes  
After gathering some initial requirements data, we showed small groups of students our basic wireframes for key features like the profile page, listings of their skills, and the balance tracker for credits, gaining their feedback to check if the platform felt intuitive, and if the credit system was inspiring and motivating, or unclear and confusing.  

### 5. Persona Development  
Based upon the data we gained from surveys, interviews and focus groups, we developed a small set of “personas", to represent a wide range of students from different backgrounds. The personas then provided guidance for design decisions and kept us grounded in real student contexts.  

---

## Research Results and Implications  
The study produced a demand for skill exchange of both academic skills and practical skills, as tutoring, proofreading and help moving all received the highest attention.  

Again, students were clearly concerned about trust and reliability, which reaffirms a need for peer reviews, ratings and visible credit histories on the database. Flexibility was also found to be important, as many students had unpredictable tutoring work schedules, so availability management is an important feature to include.  

In general, the findings demonstrate students are willing to engage in a credit-based skill exchange, but the success of the platform relies on making it easy to use, trustworthy and flexible.

## Competitor Analysis  

### Timebank  
Timebank is a community-based scheme, where individuals swap services using "time credits." One hour of work equals one credit, whatever the service, which is very similar to how FUSS will operate. The appealing feature of this scheme is that you can access a fair and equitable exchange, which has allowed the scheme to succeed in various UK communities for many years, and has increased levels of volunteerism (Timebanking UK, n.d.).  

However, Timebank is not specifically for students. Most of the activities are community-based and mostly consist of errands and gardening. The digital tools that support TimeBanking tend to be very basic. You will not find important features like peer-evaluations, detailed and comprehensive profiles, calendars to track current and future availability, and other options that would streamline the exchange of time credits.  

The main lesson to learn from Timebank is the model of one credit equals one hour, does work to produce community engagement however if FUSS is to be a benefit to students the digital tools need to be much better.  

### Airtasker  
Airtasker is a task outsourcing marketplace where people post jobs such as moving furniture, cleaning jobs/design work and others bid to take it on. It has a broad spectrum of categories and a defined process to post a job request, work through the details, and leave a review afterwards. This structure makes it easy to order tasks and establish trust between strangers (BetterTeam, 2024).  

The downside is that Airtasker is money-driven, the tasks are paid for by people which means most students won’t be able to post tasks, and to get a task you need to competitively bid against others. This commercial element makes it closer to freelancing than a community venture.  

For FUSS, Airtasker has shown us the value of clear workflows (posting a job, messaging, confirming job completion) and a reputation system, but reinforced our sense that a credit based, non-financial model is better for a student-to-student context.  

### Tandem  
Tandem is a language exchange application that connects people who want to learn each other's languages. The model is very relevant to FUSS by being based on mutual benefit, you teach your language and practice another in return. The application also has useful features such as matching users based on their goals, and it has an in-app messaging system (Lingualid, 2025; Tandem, n.d.).  

Tandem struggles in its scope and reliability. Its focus is limited to language learning, which limits the target audience. Some users also report that their partners lose interest, or do not commit to regular sessions, which is a consistent problem when using peer-to-peer exchange.  

Tandem teaches FUSS that a good matching system is important, flexible communication is needed and that engagement and accountability are also important for ensuring users have a good and long-lasting experience.
